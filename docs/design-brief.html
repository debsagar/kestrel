1. What Kestrel Audio is, and the network it runs on

Kestrel Audio is a fictional mid-size consumer-electronics brand: earbuds and a portable speaker, assembled in China from imported components and sold into Europe through three regional distribution centers. The whole company — its name, suppliers, customers, prices, and every business rule — is invented for this simulator, deliberately built to look and behave like a real importer/distributor's operation (the mechanisms below are source-backed), without claiming to reproduce any real company's actual contracts, suppliers, or numbers.

Network nodes: one PLANT (assembly, Dongguan) feeding DC-NL (Rotterdam, the only ocean/air gateway) by ocean (Suez ~30-day mean transit, Cape ~42-day mean, both with a standard deviation modelling schedule variance) or air/sea-air; DC-NL then feeds DC-DE and DC-PL by rail or truck (1-3 days), with a direct DC-DE↔DC-PL truck lane for rebalancing. Four customers hang off the DCs: C-BIGBOX (35% share, DC-DE, 3%-of-order-value late/short penalty), C-MARKET (30%, DC-DE, tiered ASN-accuracy-style penalty), C-PLCHAIN (20%, DC-PL, a flat 2%-of-order-value late-delivery penalty), C-WEB (15%, DC-DE, no penalty — direct-to-consumer). All three penalty bases are order value, not COGS (kestrel/fulfilment.py::_chargeback) — see §7 on why that basis is a Kestrel choice, not a reproduction of the real programs that inspired it. Six factors are simulated end to end: procurement, logistics, plant (assembly), distribution, fulfilment, and finance.

2. One order's journey, end to end (real IDs, runs/red_sea/rule/run.json)

Kestrel's "order" record is the customer-facing sales order; it is fed from pooled DC stock, not tied 1:1 to any single inbound purchase order — exactly like a real distributor's DC. To show the full chain honestly, this section traces both halves that meet at the DC: the inbound component/assembly journey that builds stock, and a customer order that draws on it. They are not the same document chain; the simulator does not claim they are.

2a. Inbound: PO-0001, a battery purchase order

Day 0 — created, straight to in_production: 50,000-unit-tier price of €1.50/unit, 74,250 units confirmed, 30% deposit (€33,412.50) paid immediately, FOB incoterm, promised day 10.
Day 10 — shipped (ASN-0001 issued): 74,250 units shipped, carrier-estimated arrival day 11.
Day 11 — received (GR-0001 goods receipt).
Day 12 — inspected (LOT-0001 passes incoming sampling).
Day 15 — invoiced → matched → paid in the same day (INV-0001): balance €77,962.50 paid, 3-way match against the PO and goods receipt clears automatically.

2b. Outbound: SO-0117, a speaker order caught by the Red Sea diversion

Same run, same 45-day red_sea task. On day 3 a scenario override force-diverts the Suez lane; the public news item (visible to both players, day 6) reads:

"Asia-Europe services rerouted via the Cape of Good Hope — Transit times extend by 10-14 days on the Cape of Good Hope route; war-risk surcharge applies." (kind: "lane")
Day 21 — order SO-0117 received: customer C-PLCHAIN, 233 units of SPK-1, requested day 26.
Day 28 — allocated (stock only became available after the diverted inbound booking finally cleared — 7 days later than requested).
Day 29 — shipped, day 31 — delivered: data.late = true, invoice AR-0147 raised for €20,737.00. C-PLCHAIN's contract term ("late2") is a flat 2%-of- order-value late-delivery penalty — a Kestrel-specific, fictional basis (see §7) — so a chargeback fires: CB-0001, created day 31, €414.74 (exactly 2% of €20,737.00). This is the only chargeback in the entire 45-day run — it is also exactly the day-45 P&L chargebacks total quoted in §4.

4. How disruptions cascade (the Red Sea example, traced through frames)

  1. Exogenous shock (day 3, red_sea task's own override): the Suez lane is forced into a diverted state. This is the only place the task injects anything that isn't organic engine dynamics — every other consequence below is the plant/logistics/ distribution/finance code reacting exactly as it would to a real diversion.
  2. First observable effect (day 6): the public "Asia-Europe services rerouted..." news item above — the only channel either player has for the cause; there is no "lane state" field the players can read directly.
  3. Bookings roll (days 12, 16, 18): BK-0004 rolled to cutoff day 18, BK-0007 to day 22, BK-0009 to day 24 — each a public exception (kind: "booking") with the booking's own ID and new cutoff day.
  4. Transit stretches (booking BK-0002): booked day 3, loaded day 11, carrier-estimated arrival day 56 — a 45-day transit versus the un-diverted ~30-day Suez mean, consistent with the diversion's stated "extend by 10-14 days" plus queueing, and directionally matching the Cape-routing evidence in docs/research/freight-and-ports.md ("Cape of Good Hope... total time to 40 to 50 days").
  5. Stock and order impact: DC-NL/DC-DE inbound stock arrives later than the un-diverted plan; order SO-0117 (§2b) waits a week past its requested day for allocation and delivers late.
  6. Financial impact (day-45 P&L, this run): cash €1,923,449.28; revenue €2,790,087.00; freight €-66,975.22; surcharges €-4,000.00; chargebacks €-414.74; demurrage €0.00 (the rule planner avoided demurrage entirely in this run — see docs/model-findings.md for the luna-model comparison, once generated).

5. The decisions the planner (rule-based or model) actually makes

Every action below is a real engine boundary call (kestrel/agent/tools.py's ENGINE_TOOLS, mirrored in frontend/app.js's ACTIONS table) — nothing is generated prose:

  • create_po / cancel_po / expedite_po — what to buy, from whom, how much, and whether to pay an air-freight premium to pull a shipment in.
  • set_inspection_level (I/II/III) / qualify_supplier / request_audit — how hard to sample incoming lots, and whether to bring the backup SoC supplier (S-BRK) online or trigger a for-cause supplier audit.
  • accept_invoice / dispute_invoice — whether an incoming invoice's mismatch against its PO is worth disputing.
  • return_lot — send a rejected incoming lot back rather than sorting it.
  • book_container (ocean/air/sea_air, Suez or Cape route, spot or contract rate) / cancel_booking / sign_freight_contract — how to move finished goods from the plant to Rotterdam, and whether to lock in a monthly minimum-volume freight contract (only signable 1-15 May, mirroring the May-to-May ocean contract season — see §6).
  • release_work_order / cancel_work_order — what to assemble and how much, against the plant's fixed daily capacity.
  • create_transfer — rebalance finished-goods stock between DCs.
  • set_allocation_policy / allocate_order / decline_order — priority order or fair-share allocation across customers when stock is short, and individual order overrides.
  • markdown — a temporary price cut on a SKU, trading margin for sell-through.

7. Fictional parameters (a Kestrel choice, not a real company's number)

Per the project's own evidence-audit ruling (docs/research/2026-09-15-evidence-audit.md):

  • Customer penalty rates and bases — C-BIGBOX's 3%-of-order-value late/short penalty and C-MARKET's tiered penalty are Kestrel-invented contract terms. They are inspired by real retailer OTIF/ASN compliance programs (Walmart, Amazon) but do not reproduce either company's actual published rate or basis — the research note itself flags this as "misapplied" if read as a faithful reproduction.
  • Tax — a flat duty/VAT treatment is a simplifying choice; TARIC classification is per-product, origin-dependent, and updated daily in reality (§ evidence audit, "0% EU duty" finding).
  • Demurrage tiers — the free-time-then-tiered-euro-per-day schedule is a scenario choice built from directional Rotterdam/industry ranges, not a published carrier tariff.
  • Reliability probabilities — e.g. the baseline ocean on-time probability, supplier-recovery-after-CNY-disruption probability, and container-roll probabilities are calibration choices built from broader industry statistics (global schedule-reliability indices, qualitative worker-turnover commentary), not route- or supplier-specific measured rates.
  • Sampling approximations — the AQL-to-sample-size mapping used for incoming inspection is a secondary-sourced approximation of ISO 2859-1, not the verified primary table.

8. What the simulator excludes

  • No real company, brand, supplier, or customer — every entity in §1 is invented.
  • No true product-specific tariff/HS classification, currency conversion, or multi-currency accounting — one currency (EUR), one simplified duty treatment.
  • No warehouse labor, equipment, or physical layout constraints — DC capacity is not separately modelled beyond stock levels and the inbound queue.
  • No real carrier names, specific vessel schedules, or actual contract rates — freight cost is a parametric function of mode/route/season (kestrel/network.py::LEGS), not a live rate feed.
  • No true geographic routing — the network map in the operations desk (and this brief) is schematic, not a projection.
  • No insurance, legal, regulatory-compliance, or HR/staffing modelling.
  • No hidden-state leakage to either player: true supplier health, exact freight-rate regime, port congestion queue depth, and other random-number internals are engine-only (kestrel/screens.py::HIDDEN) and never appear in a screen, a frame, or this artifact's embedded data — only their downstream, already-public effects do (confirmed quantities, ship-day slips, lot outcomes, invoice mismatches, booking rolls, demurrage, scorecards).
